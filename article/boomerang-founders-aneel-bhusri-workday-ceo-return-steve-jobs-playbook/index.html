Boomerang Founders: Why Aneel Bhusri Returned to Workday and Studied the Steve Jobs Playbook

Workday's co-founder came back as CEO in February 2026 and is rebuilding the company like a start-up. Here is why founders return and whether it works.

The Empire Insight Official Cover Feature
The Empire Insight Editorial Board November 13, 2026 • Spotlight: Aneel Bhusri (Co-founder & CEO, Workday) • Business & Leadership
Leadership Trend 2026

There is a new pattern in corporate leadership, and it has a nickname: the boomerang founder. These are entrepreneurs who built a company, handed the CEO job to a professional operator, and then came back when the business faced a threat that only a founder seemed able to fix. The clearest 2026 example is Aneel Bhusri, who co-founded Workday in 2005 and returned as CEO on February 9, 2026, effective immediately. In October, he told Fortune that he had studied Steve Jobs's comeback at Apple before his restart. This guide explains who is coming back, why, what the playbook looks like, and what it means for employees, investors and anyone building a company in the age of AI.

What Happened at Workday

Bhusri co-founded Workday with his mentor Dave Duffield in 2005 and ran it for many years. In early 2024 he handed the CEO role to co-CEO Carl Eschenbach and stayed on as executive chair, focusing on product and technology. At the time, he felt his work securing the company's future was complete, according to Fortune's CEO Daily.

Then the environment shifted. On February 9, 2026, Workday announced that Eschenbach was stepping down and Bhusri was returning as CEO immediately. Fortune noted that these were not ordinary times: the rise of AI agents was reshaping enterprise software, and investors were questioning how traditional software-as-a-service companies would fare. One industry analysis, from SaaStr, said Bhusri did not especially want to return, but that the pressure on software companies made it hard to say no, and reported that his stake had lost around $1.3 billion in paper value in less than two years.

In an interview, Bhusri said he came back to help Workday lead again during what he called the biggest technology transformation of our lives, and that he wanted to return the company to a start-up orientation and a growth mindset.

“ Professional managers are brilliant at optimizing what exists. Founders are sometimes needed to decide what should exist next. — The Empire Insight Editorial Board

The Steve Jobs Playbook Bhusri Studied

Jobs is the most famous boomerang founder in business history. He was pushed out of Apple in 1985 and returned in 1997 to a company in what Fortune described as a death spiral, so close to bankruptcy that it accepted a $150 million investment from rival Microsoft. During his second act, Apple became one of the most innovative and successful product companies in the world and launched the iPhone.

Bhusri told Fortune that he studied this return extensively before his own restart, so intensely that, by his own account, he drove his management team crazy by sending them Instagram clips of Jobs. His biggest takeaway came from an interview in which Jobs described his ambition to make Apple the biggest start-up in the world. For Jobs, that meant restoring a culture of ownership, accountability, speed and focus, and slashing a sprawling product line down to the few products that mattered most.

Bhusri said Jobs was not just a product genius but a management genius, in the way he organized Apple for success, and that he is trying to bring the same mentality of recreating a start-up mindset with clear accountability.

What Bhusri Has Done Since Returning

Based on interviews and coverage, several concrete moves stand out.

  • A re-founding strategy. Bhusri frames Workday's current phase as a re-founding moment, often called Chapter 4, built around AI agents. He has said that with AI, the company is essentially a start-up again.
  • Fewer, owned AI projects. Fortune reported that, inspired by Jobs's cuts to Apple's product line, Bhusri reduced the number of AI agents in development from 50 to 20 and gave each AI effort a single owner.
  • Headcount cuts. Workday cut about 2.5 percent of its workforce. Bhusri insisted the decision was not driven by cost-cutting or by AI replacing people, and said it was painful, adding that affected employees were offered individual contributor roles.
  • New AI leadership. Workday named Joel Hellermark, founder of recently acquired Sana, as chief AI officer.

Why Boomerang Founders Are Having a Moment

The SaaStr analysis described a classic pattern: a B2B company grows from nothing to a billion dollars in revenue, goes public, hires an experienced operator from a large technology company, and the founder slides into an executive chair role to focus on vision and product. It works well when conditions are stable. It works less well when a technology shift threatens the business model itself.

The argument for bringing founders back is that they have authority, speed and conviction that hired executives may lack. A founder can make a bold call without needing to build consensus from scratch, and employees often listen differently to the person who started the company. Professional operators tend to be optimizers, strong at improving existing systems, while a reinvention may call for someone willing to bet the company on something new.

The same analysis pointed to other examples, including UiPath's founder Daniel Dines returning to lead, and described the broader theme as software founders coming back as AI threatens to reshape the industry. The trend is not limited to software, but AI-driven disruption makes it especially visible there.

“ When the ground shifts under a company, the people who built the first foundation are sometimes the only ones who remember how. — The Empire Insight Editorial Board

The Risks: Why Comebacks Often Fail

For every Jobs, there are founders whose returns did not work out. A comeback carries clear risks, and a good board will weigh them before asking a founder to return.

  • Time and patience. Even Jobs's success took years to build. The SaaStr analysis noted that his best-known results arrived long after his return, and not every company has that runway.
  • Old habits. A founder may revert to the style that worked at an earlier, smaller scale, which can clash with a larger organization.
  • Morale and retention. Restructuring, such as headcount cuts, can hurt trust, even when the leader says the goal is focus and not savings.
  • Succession confusion. A founder's return can signal that the previous CEO was a mistake, which complicates the question of who leads next.
  • Dependence on one person. Putting the company's future in one founder's hands raises the same key-person risk that other companies are facing this month.

The AI and Jobs Question

Bhusri has been unusually direct about how AI could change work. In an interview with Fortune, he said AI will displace a lot of jobs within five years. In another interview he said he worries about college graduates finding work, since many entry-level jobs are the kind that AI could do, and that this is a problem society must solve.

That candor matters because Workday sells software for human resources and finance, the very systems companies use to manage their people and money. If AI agents begin doing more of the work that employees once did, the products used to manage those employees must change as well. Bhusri's view is that customers in HR and finance want a careful, controlled path to AI adoption and not a reckless one, which is the pitch Workday is making.

How This Connects to Other CEO Stories This Month

The boomerang trend sits next to a wave of other leadership stories. Apple completed a planned handover from Tim Cook to John Ternus. Google DeepMind's founder moved from CEO to chair and chief scientist. Nvidia's co-founder resigned abruptly, leaving an interim CEO in charge. Together they show a corporate world in which leadership transitions are being tested harder than usual, largely because AI is forcing every business to rethink what it does and who should lead it.

One way to see the difference is by type. Apple's was an orderly succession. DeepMind's was a role redesign that keeps the founder close. Nvidia's was a sudden exit. Workday's is a founder return. Each solves a different problem, and each shows that there is no single correct way to hand over power.

Lessons for Founders, Boards and Employees

  • Founders: Stepping back is not necessarily permanent. If you stay close as chair, you keep the option to return, and you keep the knowledge needed to do it well.
  • Boards: Define in advance what would trigger a founder's return and how a comeback would be structured, rather than improvising in a crisis.
  • CEOs: Hired operators should expect that a technology shift can change the job description overnight. Strategy documents should plan for disruption, not just optimization.
  • Employees: A founder's return usually brings faster decisions and more change. Clear communication and clear ownership of projects are the best signs the plan is working.
  • Investors: Treat a comeback as a strategic bet, not a guarantee. Look for specific milestones such as product focus, margins and customer retention.

What to Watch at Workday

  • AI agent launches. Whether the smaller set of agents reaches customers quickly and shows real adoption.
  • Financial results. Growth, margins and customer retention over the next several quarters.
  • Talent signals. Hiring, departures and how employees describe the culture shift.
  • Competitive moves. How larger software rivals and AI-native start-ups respond.
  • Leadership bench. Who emerges as Bhusri's long-term successor and what role Duffield continues to play.

The Bigger Picture

The boomerang founder is more than a fashionable story. It reflects a deeper truth about how companies age. Organizations tend to become better at repeating success and worse at changing direction, and a sudden technology shift can expose that weakness. A returning founder is one way for a company to try to rediscover urgency.

Whether the trend will deliver depends on execution, not on nostalgia. Jobs's second act worked because he combined authority with a clear strategy and a few great products. Bhusri is betting that a similar blend of focus, ownership and speed can reposition Workday for the AI era. The answer will arrive in product results and customer numbers, not in interviews. Frequently Asked Questions

What is a boomerang founder?

A boomerang founder is an entrepreneur who steps away from the CEO role, often handing it to a professional manager, and later returns to lead the company again, usually during a period of disruption.

When did Aneel Bhusri return as Workday CEO?

Workday announced on February 9, 2026, that Carl Eschenbach was stepping down and Bhusri was returning as CEO, effective immediately.

What did Aneel Bhusri learn from Steve Jobs?

He said he studied Jobs's return to Apple and took away the importance of restoring a start-up culture of ownership, accountability, speed and focus, including cutting the number of projects to those that matter most.

Did Workday lay off employees?

Workday cut about 2.5 percent of its workforce, according to coverage of Bhusri's remarks. He said the decision was not driven by cost-cutting or by AI replacing people.

Why do founders return to their companies?

Analysts say founders return when a company faces an existential or strategic threat that calls for fast, high-conviction decisions, such as the shift to AI agents in enterprise software.

Do founder comebacks usually succeed?

Results vary. Steve Jobs's return to Apple is the most famous success, but it took years, and many comebacks do not match it. Outcomes depend on strategy, timing and execution.
