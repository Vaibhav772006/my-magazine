Semiconductor Equipment Shortage: Why Chip Manufacturing Bottlenecks Are So Hard to Fix

ASML, CoWoS packaging, high-bandwidth memory and long equipment lead times: why AI demand is outrunning the machines that make the chips.

The Empire Insight Official Cover Feature
The Empire Insight Editorial Board October 22, 2026 • Technology & Industry
Industry Insight 2026

When most people hear "chip shortage," they think of 2021, when car factories sat idle because of a missing part the size of a fingernail. The new squeeze is different, and in some ways it is harder to fix. This time the pinch is not in cheap, everyday chips. It sits in the most advanced corner of the industry, where AI processors, high-bandwidth memory and the machines that build them are all competing for the same limited capacity. Here is what's going on with the semiconductor equipment shortage, why chip manufacturing bottlenecks are so stubborn, and what it means for anyone who buys technology.

This Isn't the 2021 Shortage

The pandemic-era shortage was mostly about mature chips, the older, cheaper kind that run car electronics and appliances. Demand whipsawed, supply couldn't keep up, and the shortage eased once factories adjusted.

Today's pressure comes from artificial intelligence. Training and running large AI models takes huge numbers of advanced processors, and each one needs high-bandwidth memory and specialized packaging. Research firm Omdia has raised its 2026 forecast for global chip revenue sharply and says supply bottlenecks may persist into 2027, because AI demand is growing faster than factories can expand. The shortage is less like a traffic jam and more like a highway that was never built wide enough for the cars now using it.

Why Equipment Is the Hidden Choke Point

Here's the part that surprises people. You can't simply decide to build more chips. First you need a factory, which can cost tens of billions of dollars. Then you need machines to fill it, and those machines come from a small number of specialist companies with their own limits.

The headline name is ASML, the Dutch company that is the only supplier of extreme ultraviolet (EUV) lithography machines, the tools that print the finest circuit patterns on leading-edge chips. A single EUV system is enormous, extremely complex and costs hundreds of millions of dollars. It is assembled from parts supplied by a long chain of specialist firms, including mirrors, lasers and precision components that few companies in the world can make.

ASML has said it expects to ship more than sixty EUV systems in 2026, roughly a quarter more than the year before, and it has raised its revenue outlook. It has also talked about expanding capacity by around thirty percent a year in 2027 and 2028. Even so, some analysts argue the company itself has become part of the bottleneck, simply because demand is so strong. And ASML isn't alone. Applied Materials, Lam Research, Tokyo Electron and KLA make other critical tools, and reports in 2026 suggest their delivery times have stretched sharply, in some cases to one and a half to two times what they were.

Equipment Makers Have Their Own Shortages

One of the more counterintuitive findings from industry coverage is that equipment makers aren't short of willingness or money to expand. What they lack are components and raw materials of their own. A chip-making machine is made of thousands of precise parts, and if one supplier slips, the whole machine waits.

It's a reminder that the semiconductor industry is a chain of chains. Slow down a single link, whether a specialty gas, an optical element or a vacuum component, and the delay ripples upward. Building a new supplier takes years, not months.

The Packaging and Memory Squeeze

Making the silicon is only half the job. Advanced AI processors have to be packaged together with memory, and the process most associated with this is TSMC's CoWoS, short for Chip-on-Wafer-on-Substrate. Industry sources have described CoWoS capacity as oversubscribed through at least 2026, and Nvidia has publicly said the same about its own needs.

The memory side is just as tight. High-bandwidth memory, or HBM, is stacked tightly next to AI processors, and memory makers such as SK Hynix, Samsung and Micron have reported that their output is largely allocated well in advance. SK Hynix has even said the DRAM market is expected to stay short through 2026, especially at the high end.

The consequence is that a chip designer can have a brilliant design, a wafer booked at the foundry, and still wait, because the packaging line or the memory stack isn't available. That is why many analysts now say the real constraint has moved from the wafer to everything that comes after it.

Bottlenecks Beyond the Machines

  • Skilled people. Building and running a fab needs thousands of specialized engineers and technicians, and the talent pool is thin in many regions.
  • Power and water. Advanced factories consume large amounts of both, and local infrastructure has to keep up.
  • Construction time. A new fab typically takes years from groundbreaking to meaningful output.
  • Cleanroom capacity. Even when equipment arrives, there must be a qualified space ready to install and calibrate it.
  • Qualification. New lines have to be tested and proven before they make sellable chips, which adds months more.

The Geopolitical Layer

Semiconductors are now a matter of national strategy. The United States, Europe, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, China and India are all investing heavily in domestic chip capacity. TSMC alone has committed to a very large US investment as it expands outside Taiwan, and countries such as South Korea are planning big industrial clusters.

Yet money doesn't bypass the equipment queue. Korean industry reports have raised the possibility that securing advanced equipment could become a major variable in expansion plans, even as some officials say it is too early to call the situation a bottleneck. Subsidies like the US CHIPS Act help build factories, but they don't instantly produce lithography machines or packaging lines. Export controls add another complication, because rules on what can be sold to whom change where equipment can go.

What This Means for Buyers, Businesses and Builders

If you buy computers, phones or cloud services, the effects are often indirect but real. When advanced memory and processors are scarce, prices for related components can rise, and some products launch in smaller quantities. Companies that rely on AI infrastructure may find they have to wait for capacity or pay a premium to secure it.

For businesses planning technology purchases, a few practical habits help:

  1. Order earlier. Lead times for servers and specialized hardware can be long, so build them into your timeline.
  2. Stay flexible. Where possible, qualify more than one supplier or configuration.
  3. Use what you have. Squeeze more from existing hardware with better software and scheduling before adding more.
  4. Watch the memory market. Memory pricing often shows tightness before it appears elsewhere.

Is Relief on the Way?

There are reasons for cautious optimism. TSMC is spending heavily on new capacity, and its guidance for 2026 capital spending sits in the tens of billions of dollars. ASML plans meaningful capacity growth over the next couple of years. Memory makers are expanding, and new packaging lines are coming online.

But expect relief to arrive in stages, not all at once, and expect the bottleneck to move around. When one constraint eases, attention often shifts to the next weakest link. A reasonable reading of current forecasts is that the tightest conditions will last through 2027, with gradual improvement afterward if demand doesn't outrun the new supply. Treat that as an informed estimate rather than a certainty, because demand for AI is the biggest unknown of all.

A Simple Way to Picture a Chip's Journey

Imagine ordering a custom-built car where every part comes from a different specialist, and each specialist has a waiting list. A modern AI chip is similar. A design team creates the blueprint. A foundry like TSMC prints it onto silicon wafers using lithography machines. Separate suppliers make the memory. Then the pieces are packaged together, tested, and shipped to a server maker who assembles them into systems for data centers.

Every one of those steps has its own capacity, its own lead time and its own risks. A delay in any stage holds up everything after it. That is why you can read headlines about record demand and record investment in the same week as stories about shortages. Both can be true, because spending money is much faster than building the physical capacity that the money is meant to buy.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is causing the semiconductor equipment shortage?

Strong AI-driven demand for advanced chips, combined with long lead times for lithography and other tools and limits in the supply chains of the equipment makers themselves.

What is a chip manufacturing bottleneck?

It is a single stage in the production process, such as lithography, packaging or memory supply, that limits how many finished chips can be made no matter how strong other stages are.

Why is ASML so important?

ASML is the only company that makes EUV lithography machines, which are essential for producing the most advanced chips.

How long will the shortage last?

Analysts such as Omdia expect bottlenecks to persist into 2027, though timing depends on how fast new capacity comes online and how AI demand evolves.

Key Takeaways • Chip Manufacturing Bottlenecks

Today's shortage is concentrated in advanced AI chips, not everyday components.

Equipment makers like ASML face their own component and capacity limits.

Packaging and high-bandwidth memory are as tight as chip fabrication itself.

Money alone cannot shorten the years it takes to build fabs and tools.

Expect gradual relief, with the bottleneck shifting from one link to the next.
