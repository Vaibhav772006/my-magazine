NASA Voyager 1: How Far Has Humanity's Most Distant Spacecraft Travelled?

Voyager 1 hit one light-day from Earth on November 18, 2026. Here is what 16 billion miles means, how fast it flies and what is next.

The Empire Insight Official Cover Feature
The Empire Insight Editorial Board November 20, 2026 • Spotlight: NASA Voyager 1 (Interstellar Probe, NASA) • Science & Space
Space Watch

On November 18, 2026, NASA's Voyager 1 reached a distance no human-made object has ever touched: one light-day from Earth. That is the distance light travels in 24 hours, about 16.1 billion miles, or 25.9 billion kilometres. The spacecraft launched in 1977 with less computing power than a modern car key, and it is still talking to us. Here is how far it has gone, what a light-day really means, and what comes next.

How Far Has Voyager 1 Travelled?

According to NASA's calculations, Voyager 1 crossed the one light-day mark at 5:16 a.m. Eastern Time on November 18, 2026 (about 3:46 p.m. in India). At that moment it was roughly 16.094 billion miles (25.902 billion kilometres) away, which is about 173 astronomical units, where one astronomical unit is the Earth-sun distance. It took 49 years, 2 months and 11 days to get there.

A small catch: because a radio signal itself needs a day to cross that gap, NASA's team has to wait roughly a day for a signal from the probe to confirm the milestone. The date comes from calculation first and confirmation second.

What Is a Light-Day?

Light moves at about 300,000 kilometres per second. Over 24 hours, that adds up to the 25.9 billion kilometres above. The Moon, by comparison, is only about 1.3 light-seconds away. When Artemis II astronauts made their trip, NASA noted it took them three days to cover the distance light crosses in about a second. Voyager 1 is out where a command sent from mission control takes a full day to arrive, and the reply takes another day to return.

Voyager 1 by the Numbers

  • Launched: September 5, 1977, two weeks after its twin, Voyager 2.
  • Speed: almost 38,000 miles per hour (about 61,000 km/h).
  • Interstellar space: crossed the heliopause, the edge of the sun's influence, in 2012.
  • Round-trip signal time: about 48 hours.
  • Mission link: the Deep Space Network, NASA's global antenna system, keeps the connection alive.

Voyager 1 also overtook Pioneer 10 in February 1998 to become the most distant human-made object, a title it has held for more than 28 years.

Is Voyager 1 Still Working?

Yes, but carefully. The probe runs on a radioactive power source that produces a little less electricity every year, so the engineering team has been switching off instruments and heaters one by one to keep the essentials alive. Only a few science instruments still operate, including a plasma wave instrument and a magnetometer that keep sampling the space between the stars. NASA estimates the spacecraft could drop out of the Deep Space Network's range around 2036, depending on how much power it has left to transmit.

That is part of why this milestone matters. It's a story about persistence as much as distance. Mission managers have fixed software glitches from billions of miles away, nursed thrusters back to life and kept a 1970s machine in conversation with Earth. What About Voyager 2?

Voyager 2 is heading a different way and travels more slowly. NASA expects it to reach the one light-day mark in November 2035, and even optimistic estimates question whether it will still be operating by then. Both probes carry the Golden Record, a gold-plated disc of sounds and images meant as a message for anyone who might one day find it.

Where Is Voyager 1 Headed?

It is cruising toward a star called Gliese 445 (also catalogued as AC+79 3888), which is more than 17 light-years away. At its current speed, the trip will take tens of thousands of years, so this journey is about the long view.
