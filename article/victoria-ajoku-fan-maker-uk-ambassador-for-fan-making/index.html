Victoria Ajoku: The UK Fan Maker Giving an Ancient Craft a Bold New Future

How the UK Ambassador for Fan Making and founder of Fan The Glory With Tori is reviving a critically endangered heritage craft through mobile workshops across the United Kingdom.

The Empire Insight Official Cover Feature
The Empire Insight Editorial Board November 5, 2026 • Spotlight: Victoria Ajoku (Master of Arts, Master Fan Maker, Fan Curator & UK Ambassador for Fan Making) • Creative Entrepreneurship & Heritage Crafts
Creative Entrepreneur • UK Heritage Crafts

What happens to a craft when fewer people learn it, fewer people practise it, and a whole generation grows up barely knowing it exists? For fan making in the UK, that moment arrived in 2017. For Victoria Ajoku, it was not a sad statistic but a call to action.

A UK Craft That Was Running Out of Time

In 2017, fan making was classified as a critically endangered craft. Many people would have read that and moved on. Victoria asked herself a different question: how could she use her business to give fan making a future?

Today, Master of Arts, Master Fan Maker and Fan Curator Victoria Ajoku is the UK Ambassador for Fan Making. Her answer is Fan The Glory With Tori, a UK company that teaches, makes, performs and champions the hand fan for a new audience. It rests on skill and cultural heritage, and on a simple belief: an ancient craft can stay meaningful if someone is willing to learn it, teach it and let it evolve.

From Curiosity to Calling

Victoria's story started quietly. In 2012, she began collecting souvenir hand fans while teaching herself to make accessories by hand, jewellery included. It was creative curiosity and a love of sustainable, handmade crafts, nothing more at the time.

Her master's degree in performance and creative research gave that curiosity a direction. She loved working with props on stage and decorating theatre set designs, which taught her to see a fan as both a practical performance tool and an object with real visual and cultural weight. Her West African heritage made it personal too, since she had known African Chieftaincy fans since childhood.

In 2015 she turned professional. She taught, made handmade fans to sell and took bespoke fan commissions, and those early years laid the foundation for everything that followed.

The Timing That Changed Everything

In 2016, the Heritage Crafts organisation in the UK began gathering research on crafts that are viable, endangered or critically endangered. The aim was to shine a light on skills at risk of disappearing and on the people working to protect them. A year later, fan making landed in the red zone as critically endangered.

Victoria felt the craft needed a new lease of life, and a new voice, face and business to carry it into the modern era. She sensed a deep calling to take on that role.

Filling a Gap in the UK Fan-Making Sector

Early in her career, Victoria noticed that two groups were largely missing from fan making: young people and people from ethnic backgrounds. Looking at the centre stage of the UK sector, she could not find anyone who looked and thought the way she did. So she decided to help fill that space herself.

Her background with fans in dance and singing performance brings the craft to life on stage as well as at the workbench. She describes that pairing as rare, and one that only she offers through Fan The Glory With Tori. For Victoria, representation is more than a talking point. It means turning up, lending a positive voice to the sector and showing that fan making has room for anyone who wants to try it.

Fanning the Way to Freedom: Mobile Fan Making Workshops in the UK

Her company slogan, Fanning the Way to Freedom, sums up what she wants for the craft: free of gatekeeping, free to grow and free for anyone to try.

Victoria runs a mobile service, so she brings fan making workshops to people where they are, in community and public settings the craft has rarely reached. Her workshops are inclusive, fun, educational, entertaining and therapeutic, a mix she says she had not seen from any other fan maker or fan-related company in the UK. People of all ages, backgrounds and abilities can try the craft in a welcoming and safe space.

Her reasons are plain. Fan-making events, she says, have long been organised by the same people in the same venues year after year, so the same voices keep getting heard. Over time that pattern has pushed aside the fans of the African continent, among others, and left the impression that the craft belongs to a select few. Fans are made and used on every continent, and Victoria believes no endangered craft can thrive behind closed doors.

Where Heritage Meets Innovation

Victoria keeps tradition alive by pairing heritage with modern craftsmanship. She teaches classes and sells eco-friendly folding hand fans in a range of sizes, balancing the old with the contemporary.

She was also the first fan maker in the UK to bring paper marbling to handmade folding fans. Paper marbling is an endangered craft in its own right, so the result is something genuinely new: the marbled hand fan, one object that carries two threatened traditions.

“No endangered craft can thrive behind closed doors.— Victoria Ajoku

Leading Through Collaboration

Victoria calls herself a sustainable and innovative leader and a humble solopreneur who is open to collaboration. More than half of her work happens in partnership with other leaders, charities, organisations and businesses. Her style balances autonomy, alliance and what she calls “people empowerment.”

She is also the face of her own brand. She runs every workshop and makes the fan accessories herself, which keeps quality and authenticity intact. She is confident that nobody does what she does in quite the same way.

Building a Brand People Remember

Turning a craft into a business meant finding several ways to get in front of people. Performance, from fan dancing to fan singing, helps her stand out, and networking keeps her in the room. She gives talks, runs demonstrations at craft events and attends business gatherings, always with business cards and QR codes in hand. Social media lets her share and archive her work as it happens.

Her calendar runs well ahead of her. Most collaborations are planned a year in advance and backed by extensive research and field work, and coverage in newspapers, magazines and on television has widened her audience further.

Lessons From the Hard Moments

The road has not been easy. In the early years, Victoria faced discrimination that left her on the margins, and finding a place where her skills and her company were welcomed and supported was hard.

That experience taught her to build her own tribe, a circle within the communities where she could be celebrated. Genuine supporters came from it. She now believes no one is ever truly alone, because a support system can simply be redirected. She also learned to gather people to mark her wins, awards, achievements, scholarships and business anniversaries, because the impact an organisation makes deserves to be recognised and applauded.

Her Advice to Makers and Small Business Owners: Come As You Are

In the classroom, Victoria starts with authenticity. An artisan cannot be separated from the work they make, she says, so makers should let their real selves lead their craft. Trouble tends to start when people imitate someone else or copy a single model that worked for another person. No one formula fits everyone, and makers have to believe in their own products and abilities, or nobody else will.

  • Show up, calm and confident. The right connections can pass by unnoticed.
  • Keep learning. Read, research, try other crafts and take classes in your own field from different teachers.
  • Talk to other makers. Fellow artists and craftspeople share experience you cannot get alone.
  • Solve problems to build skill. It also makes winning clients more likely.
  • Be easy to find locally and online. Have a website, a working phone number and a social media presence.

For anyone starting a business without a business qualification, she offers reassurance. Preparation and knowledge are valuable, but she has found that real-life experience of running a business is what you truly need. Her shortest piece of advice? “Come as you are.”

UK Recognition and International Reach

Victoria is proud and thankful for every award and television appearance. Her screen credits include the BBC's The Repair Shop on the Road, Channel 4's Steph's Packed Lunch and Love Your Weekend with Alan Titchmarsh. She has also been featured in Forbes, i News, The Sunday Telegraph, The Elite X and The Executive Lens. Her teaching has taken her abroad, to Spain and to Amsterdam for the Ambacht en Bleed festival.

The Road Ahead for UK Fan Making

Victoria's ambitions as UK Ambassador for Fan Making are broad. She would like to see fan making move into fresh territory such as the beauty industry, ceramics, advertising and industrial sectors. As an educator, she is designing a syllabus that would let learners earn a certificate in fan crafting, so the craft is respected as a profession and not dismissed as a pastime.

Her work as a Fan Curator is growing as well. She has already curated two fan exhibitions in open public spaces, and she enjoys the influence of gallery curation, which lets her share history and perspectives while uplifting a space. Her goal is to bring the right investors on board so Fan The Glory With Tori can grow into a sustainable company.

Digital platforms are part of the plan. Within five to ten years, she expects more makers of handcrafted products to team up with like-minded influencers on YouTube, TikTok and Instagram to market and sell their work to an international audience.

A Guardian of the Craft

Behind every plan is one idea: critically endangered crafts must honour the past while staying open to change. Victoria describes herself as a custodian and guardian of fan making, and she wants it to stay fresh and current so the next generation can carry it forward.

Fan making was once a craft with few makers and fewer champions. Today it has Victoria Ajoku, and a growing community learning that an old craft can still make a lot of positive noise. She is, without doubt, a true overcomer and a passionate businesswoman, persevering to preserve the heritage craft of fan making.
